Thread

You never see the maze. You only write the rules that walk it.

How to play ▼

You do not solve the maze. You write a program that solves the maze, then watch it run in the dark. The maze is only revealed where your bot has been.

The rules

Your program is an ordered list. Every tick, the bot reads the list from the top and runs the first rule whose conditions are all true. That is the whole language - order is your only control flow, so a rule near the bottom only fires when everything above it fails.

What the bot can sense

Only three things: the walls of the square it is standing on, its own memory of where it has already walked, and its flags. It has no map. Directions are relative to the way it faces, so right means the bot's right, not east.

It can also see through a doorway, but no further. Is the exit and is the key only ever become true for a square the bot has an open way into, which is the same moment that square stops being dark on the board. It cannot spot either one through a wall, or from across the maze.

Memory comes in two kinds. A way is the gap between two squares, and the bot counts how many times it has walked through each one. A square is a place, and it counts how often it has stood there. In the menus, any condition with the word way in it reads the passage; every other one reads the square. Which of the two you lean on is up to you.

Ticks

One rule fires per tick and every tick costs a step, including turns and flag changes. Moving into a wall turns the bot to face that way but gets it nowhere - and still costs a step. The run ends when the bot reaches the exit, when no rule matches, when it stops finding new ground, or when it runs out of steps.

Scoring

Two numbers: steps taken and rules used. Solving is the floor. Solving in fewer steps, with fewer rules, is the game. Gauntlet runs your program against five freshly generated mazes it has never seen, which is the real test of whether you wrote a strategy or just got lucky.

Why it is called Thread

Theseus went into the Labyrinth to kill the Minotaur, and the monster was never the hard part. Getting back out was. So Ariadne gave him a ball of thread to unwind behind him as he walked, and that thin unwinding line became the only thing in the whole maze that knew where he had been.

Your bot is in the same position. It has no map and no view of the walls ahead, only a record of the ground it has already covered, laid down one step at a time behind it. The gold line drawn across the board is that thread, and every condition in your rules that asks about a way already trodden is your bot reading it.

There is a small piece of etymology tangled up in this. The ball of thread had its own name in older English: a clew. When the word drifted from meaning a literal ball of yarn to meaning anything at all that guides you out of a confusion, the spelling drifted with it, and we ended up with clue. Every clue ever followed is a thread being wound back in.

Leaving a trail behind you turns out to be more than a story. Done properly it is provably enough to escape any maze that exists, however tangled, without ever seeing it. Working out what "properly" means is the game.
